Six Months, Flat

The account started at $6,000 and sits at $6,076. That single fact hides everything that actually happened — 5,850 signals, 420 real trades, one bad week that took $1,414 in six sessions, and a system that is measurably better now than the money suggests. This is the honest version, month by month.

June
The drawdown
Real money−$1,088

Six red days in a row

Between 5 and 12 June the account lost $1,414 across six sessions, every one of them red. Four of those days hit the $300 circuit breaker. It remains the worst stretch in the system's history.

What actually happened is now well understood. The market kept trending while our book is built to fade — it sells rallies and buys dips. On a day that simply keeps going, every signal fires into the move and every stop gets hit. Skew Charm, our best setup, was 74% of the loss at a 40% win rate.

The setups were not broken. They were doing exactly what they were built to do, in the one market condition where that is the wrong thing to do.

July

Real money+$542

One trading day out of twenty-two

July is the strangest row in the ledger: 1,028 signals logged, four real trades, one live session — and it made $542.

The system was deliberately dark while we tore into the June drawdown. That month produced the execution fix, the capture measurement, and the discovery that our simulated results matched real broker fills to within 89% — closing the question of whether the losses were a signal problem or a plumbing problem. They were neither. They were a market problem.

It also produced a quieter and more expensive discovery, one we only understood in August: a month spent switched off costs real money.

Five clean sessions, and three real defects found

Trading resumed on 10 August. Five sessions, +$427 — about $85 a day.

More importantly, August is when the system started catching its own faults. A trade that filled at the broker while the software did not notice for 58 minutes. A safety routine that counted one exit twice and cancelled a live stop. A monitor that had been watching the trades we reject and never once asked whether the setups we allow were still earning — which is how one setup quietly stopped working in May and was not noticed until August.

Each of those is now fixed, audited, and covered by a check that runs without anyone watching.

So where did the money go?

The portal shows roughly $22,658 of theoretical profit across every signal ever logged. The account moved $76. Every dollar of that difference is accounted for:

Two lines matter more than the rest. $7,165 of that theoretical profit happened before the account traded a single contract — it was never available. And $3,133 was left on sessions when the system was switched off.

Of the final $1,049, only $187 is execution. The other $862 came from seven days traded under old filter versions the current one would reject.

And the $710 in that last row is the statement era only. The account also lost $634 before 14 May, in the months when the first live version was still being shaken out — which is why the all-time figure is $76 and not $710.

The flat line is not a treadmill. It is what building looks like when you stop to fix things and only trade on 37 of 120 possible days.

Every era has beaten the one before it: −$694 → −$298 → +$39 → +$85 a day. Excluding June, the other 17 live sessions made +$1,798, about $106 a day.

The filter that starts trading tomorrow makes $2,372 a month in simulation against the old one's $1,718, on 250 fewer trades, with the worst month improving from −$1,028 to +$530 and the worst drawdown cut by 43%. The June week that cost $1,414 would have cost $465.

The bottleneck has moved. It was the signals, then the execution — both are now measured and fine. It is now uptime. The projection assumes 21 sessions a month and we have been trading six to eleven. A perfect system returns a third of its number if you run it a third of the time.
